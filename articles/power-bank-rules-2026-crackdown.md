If you have flown this year, you may have noticed a flight attendant asking passengers to keep power banks out of the overhead bins, or a gate announcement about portable chargers you had never heard before. You are not imagining it. 2026 is the year the rules on flying with power banks changed more than they had in the previous decade — and they changed almost everywhere at once.

The short version: your power bank still belongs in your carry-on, never in checked baggage. That part has not moved. What is new is *how many* you can bring, *where* it can sit during the flight, and *whether you can use it at all* once the doors close. Below is the full picture, why it happened, and how to pack so you are not the person holding up the queue.

## The one rule that now applies almost everywhere

The biggest shift came from the International Civil Aviation Organization (ICAO), the UN body whose safety standards filter down into national law across 193 countries. Effective **27 March 2026**, ICAO set the first coordinated international standard on carrying portable batteries in the cabin. Three things matter most:

- **Two power banks per passenger.** Not a soft guideline — a hard cap on international flights.
- **No recharging the power bank in flight**, and no using it to charge your phone or laptop on board.
- **Carry-on only, and out of the overhead bin.** It has to stay somewhere you can see and reach it: a seat pocket, under the seat in front, or on your person.

The logic behind all three is the same: keep the battery *visible and reachable*. A power bank that starts smoking in your lap gets dealt with in seconds. One sealed inside a bag in a closed overhead bin is discovered only when smoke starts pouring out — by which point the fire is already self-sustaining.

This is the floor. Individual airlines and countries have stacked stricter rules on top, and that is where travellers get caught out.

## Airlines went further — and they did not all go the same way

The ICAO standard is the baseline, but carriers are free to be tougher, and through late 2025 and 2026 many were. The result is a patchwork, so the safe assumption is that your specific airline may be stricter than the global rule. A few examples show the range:

- **The overhead-bin ban.** United moved first among the big US carriers, barring power banks from overhead bins from **1 March 2026** after a charger caught fire in an overhead locker on a different airline's flight. The battery now has to ride in your seat pocket, under-seat bag, or pocket.
- **The two-bank cap, enforced.** American and Delta both brought in a firm limit of **two power banks, each under 100Wh, kept within reach**, from **1 May 2026** — bringing US domestic policy roughly in line with the international norm.
- **The single-bank carriers.** A handful go further still: Southwest, for instance, limits passengers to just **one** power bank.
- **The total in-flight ban.** Europe's Lufthansa Group — which also covers SWISS, Austrian, Brussels Airlines, Eurowings, ITA and others — banned *using or charging* power banks on board entirely from **15 January 2026**. You can carry two, but they stay switched off and stowed for the whole flight.

You can see the pattern: every one of these rules pushes in the same direction — fewer batteries, kept low, kept off, kept reachable. If you fly a mix of carriers, check each one rather than assuming the rule you followed last month still applies. ## Some countries enforce it nationwide

It is not only airlines. Several governments now mandate power bank rules across every carrier flying their routes, which means the restriction follows the *destination*, not just the airline logo on your boarding pass:

- **Japan** enforces a nationwide in-flight ban on power bank use — no charging your devices from one, and no charging the bank itself from a seat outlet — with a two-bank, under-160Wh limit, from **April 2026**.
- **Australia** prohibits in-flight use entirely, caps you at two banks in the cabin, and bans overhead-bin storage.
- **India** requires power banks switched off, kept on your person, and out of overhead lockers on all carriers on its routes.

The takeaway is not to memorise every jurisdiction, but to check *both* ends of your trip. A charger you used freely on the way out can be a problem on the flight home. Our [country customs pages](https://canitakethis.co/) cover restricted-item rules for the 85 destinations in our database.

## The numbers that still decide everything: watt-hours

Underneath all the new rules, the capacity limits are unchanged — and they are what security actually measures. Power banks are rated in **watt-hours (Wh)**, and there are three tiers:

- **Under 100Wh** (roughly 27,000mAh at 3.7V): allowed in carry-on without airline approval. This covers almost every phone and laptop charger sold.
- **100Wh to 160Wh**: allowed **only with prior airline approval**, and capped at two spare batteries.
- **Over 160Wh**: banned from passenger aircraft completely — cabin and hold alike.

Most chargers list only mAh on the label, so if you are unsure, the conversion is simple: **Wh = (mAh × voltage) ÷ 1000**. A typical 20,000mAh bank at 3.7V works out to about 74Wh — comfortably legal. ## Why the sudden crackdown?

Rules this coordinated do not appear without a reason. The reason is a run of cabin fires — and the physics of the devices themselves.

Unlike a phone or laptop, a bare power bank has **no thermal management, no temperature sensors, and no software watching for a fault**. If a cell is damaged, stressed, or simply badly made, its flammable electrolyte can vaporise, build pressure, and ignite. That is *thermal runaway*, and once it starts there is no built-in system to stop it.

Three incidents in particular pushed regulators from advice to enforcement:

- In **January 2025**, a power bank stored in an overhead bin ignited on an **Air Busan** A321 on the ground in South Korea. The aircraft was destroyed beyond repair; the evacuation injured 27 people.
- In **July 2025**, a charger in an overhead locker caught fire during descent on a **Virgin Australia** flight; crew put it out in the cabin.
- In **October 2025**, a power bank burst into flames in an overhead bin mid-flight on an **Air China** service. The aircraft landed safely — and the incident directly triggered United's overhead-bin ban.

The trend behind the headlines is just as stark. The US Federal Aviation Administration logged a record **89 lithium-battery incidents in 2024**, and the pace did not slow in 2025. Industry body IATA has noted that passengers now carry an average of up to **four lithium-battery devices each** — which works out to well over 1,800 batteries on a single full A380. When that many cells share a cabin, keeping the highest-risk ones visible and reachable stops being paranoia and starts being arithmetic.

## How to pack so you sail through

Pulling it together, here is the 2026-proof approach:

1. **Power banks in your carry-on, always.** Never in checked baggage — that rule is global and absolute.
2. **Bring no more than two** (one for the strictest carriers), each ideally under 100Wh.
3. **Keep them reachable** — seat pocket, under-seat bag, or on you. Assume the overhead bin is off-limits.
4. **Don't plan to charge in flight.** On a growing list of airlines and countries you simply cannot, so charge up before you board.
5. **Protect the terminals** — original packaging, a pouch, or tape over the contacts — so a loose bank cannot short against your keys.
6. **Check both ends of your trip**, plus each airline if you are connecting. The rules now vary by carrier *and* by country.

Do that and your charger is a non-event at security. Get it wrong and, at best, you are repacking at the gate — at worst, watching an expensive battery go in the bin.
