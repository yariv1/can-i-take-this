Overbooked Flight Compensation 2026: What US Airlines Owe You for Bumping and Denied Boarding

Airline Fees·Updated October 2026·8 min read

If an airline bumps you from an overbooked flight and you did nothing wrong, US law makes it pay you 200% or 400% of your one-way fare, up to $1,075 or $2,150. It depends on how many hours later you land than you were meant to. If the replacement flight gets you there within an hour of your original arrival, nothing is owed. If you give up your seat voluntarily, the law sets no amount at all: the deal is whatever you and the airline agree.

🙋 Volunteer, or be bumped against your will?

Airlines sell more tickets than seats because some passengers never show up. When more people turn up than there are seats, the airline is required to ask for volunteers before it can bump anyone. That is the moment where you have the most power: DOT says there is no limit on the money or vouchers the airline may offer and that you are free to negotiate. An airline’s plan for volunteers is not set by law, so the offer can be a check, a voucher, or a travel certificate, and each airline decides.

If nobody volunteers, the airline picks who gets bumped using its own rules, such as check-in time, fare paid or frequent flyer status. It may not use something like race or ethnicity. Once your boarding pass has been scanned and you have been told to board, an airline generally cannot take your seat; the exceptions are safety, security or health, or disruptive behaviour.

💵 How much you get when you are bumped involuntarily

DOT sets the amount from the length of your arrival delay, measured between the flight you were meant to take and the substitute flight the airline arranges. The current caps, in the DOT page and in the federal rule, are $1,075 and $2,150; articles that still print lower caps are out of date.

If your carrier offers a voucher or a free ticket in place of the cash, it has to be worth at least the cash, the airline must tell you the exact cash amount you are giving up, and it must disclose every restriction, such as fees and blackout dates, before you decide.

You still get a seat. Even when no money is owed, Delta and United say they will put you on the next flight with space at no extra cost, and American and Southwest promise the next available flight. Delta adds a hotel when the wait is overnight.

Flying from Europe or the UK? A different rulebook

DOT’s rule applies only to flights that leave a US airport. For a flight leaving the EU, Iceland, Norway or Switzerland on any airline, or arriving in the EU on an EU airline, EU rules apply instead, and they pay a fixed sum by distance. For the UK, the rules cover flights leaving a UK airport on any airline, and flights arriving in the UK on an EU or UK airline. The Your Europe page says that if you arrived on time for check-in you should always receive compensation when you are denied boarding because of overbooking, except for reasons such as health, safety, security or missing documents. You can also choose between a refund and a new flight. The UK Civil Aviation Authority adds that, whether you volunteered or not, the airline must give you food and drink, a way to communicate and a hotel if you are re-routed the next day. If you have already received benefits for the same journey under a non-EU law, the EU rules do not add a second payment. American also publishes its own EU-to-US offer: a travel voucher or a check for 300 euros (under 4 hours late) or 600 euros (4 hours or more).

Before you fly

Check in as soon as it opens and reach the gate early. Check-in time is the first thing most airlines use to decide who is bumped, and you lose the right to compensation if you are late.
If the gate agent asks for volunteers, ask the DOT questions before you say yes: next confirmed flight, meal, hotel, voucher validity, whether it works on international flights.
If you are bumped against your will, ask for the written statement of your rights and the arrival time of the new flight.
Keep your boarding pass, the new itinerary and every receipt for meals, hotel and rides.
Flying out of Europe or the UK, note the real arrival time: compensation depends on how late you land.

❓ Quick answers

How much compensation do you get for an overbooked flight?

If you are bumped against your will from a US departure and arrive over an hour late, the airline owes 200% of your one-way fare (up to $1,075) for a delay of 1 to 2 hours on a domestic flight, and 400% (up to $2,150) for longer. For an international flight leaving the US, the line between the two is 4 hours.

What is the difference between a bumped flight and denied boarding?

None in practice. DOT uses “bumping” and “denied boarding” for the same thing: the airline cannot seat you because the flight has more passengers than seats. It is voluntary if you accept an offer to give up your seat, and involuntary if you do not.

Do I get compensation if I volunteer to give up my seat?

Whatever you and the airline agree. The law sets no amount and no limit. JetBlue says a volunteer who accepts the offer is not counted as bumped and gets no denied boarding compensation, so ask about meals, a hotel and voucher rules before you say yes.

Can I refuse a voucher and take cash?

Yes. The airline can only substitute a free or reduced ticket if its value is at least the cash, if it tells you the cash amount, and if it explains every restriction first. Southwest says in its contract that you may refuse the LUV Voucher and insist on a draft.

When do I get paid if I am bumped?

DOT says the airline must offer payment at the airport the same day. If your replacement flight leaves before the airline can pay you, it must pay within 24 hours.

Do airlines have to compensate me if the flight is cancelled?

Not under the denied boarding rule: it does not apply to a cancelled flight. If the airline cancels and you decline the alternative, you are owed a refund; see our delayed flight article for the DOT refund rule.

Does the EU pay for overbooked flights?

Yes. For a flight leaving the EU on any airline, or arriving in the EU on an EU airline, it pays 250, 400 or 600 euros by distance, half if you accept re-routing that gets you there not too late. The UK pays 220, 350 or 520 pounds, or half for a shorter delay.

What if the airline just rebooks me and I arrive within an hour?

Then nothing is owed: DOT says compensation is not due if the substitute transportation gets you to your destination or first stopover within an hour of your original arrival time.
