Food You Can Take on a Plane: Full List for TSA & International Flights (2026)

Food & Agriculture · Updated 2026 · 9 min read

Yes, you can take food on a plane. At TSA, solid food has no limit in a carry-on or a checked bag, and anything spreadable or pourable is capped at 3.4 oz (100 ml) in a carry-on. That one sentence sorts almost every food. The exceptions are frozen food, canned food, baby food, and a few things you thought were fine.

Below is every food and drink on TSA’s own “What can I bring” food list, 55 rows covering all 59 items, with the carry-on answer, the checked-bag answer, and a third column most lists skip: how likely customs is to stop it when you land in another country.

🧪 The one test that sorts every food at TSA

TSA does not judge food by what it is. It judges it by how it behaves. Under the 3-1-1 rule, a carry-on liquid, gel or paste has to fit in a 100 ml container inside one clear bag. For food, that gives a simple test.

💡
Can you spread it, pour it, pump it or scoop it? Then it is a liquid or gel. Peanut butter, jam, honey, yogurt, hummus, soup and sauce all count, even in a sealed jar. A peanut butter sandwich does not, because the bread makes it a solid. For the full rule, see the 100 ml liquids rule explained.

⚠️ The foods people get wrong

🥜
Peanut butter and nut butters are a spread. A full jar is refused at the checkpoint and goes in the checked bag.
🥫
Canned food is allowed, but TSA says cans can need extra screening and suggests packing them in the checked bag or leaving them home.
🧀
Cheese depends on the type. A block of cheddar is a solid. Cream cheese and spreadable cheese are not.
🍦
Ice cream and yogurt are on TSA’s list as 100 ml items in a carry-on.
🥪
Sandwiches, pizza, cake and pie are solids and go through fine, although an officer may ask you to take them out of the bag.

🧊 Frozen food, ice packs and dry ice

Frozen food is allowed in a carry-on and a checked bag, with one condition that decides everything: it has to be frozen solid when it reaches the checkpoint.

Ice and gel ice packs that are completely frozen are allowed.
❌
Partly melted ice, slush, or any liquid at the bottom of the cooler is refused unless it meets the 100 ml rule.
🧊
Dry ice is allowed up to 5 lb (the FAA limit), in a vented package that is marked.
💊
Medically necessary ice packs are allowed in any state. Tell the officer. See syringes, EpiPens, insulin and gel ice packs.

🍼 Baby food, formula and pouches

Baby food has its own rule. Formula, breast milk, toddler drinks and baby or toddler food, including puree pouches, are treated as medically necessary liquids. They are allowed in carry-on bags over 3.4 oz and do not have to fit in the quart bag. The full guide is baby food pouches on a plane.

👶
Tell the officer at the start of screening and take these items out of the bag to be screened separately. TSA says it may test the liquid, and you can ask for it not to be X-rayed or opened. You do not need to have the child with you.

🌴 Flying inside the US: the one fruit exception

On a domestic flight within the US mainland, fresh fruit and vegetables are fine in both bags. The exception is the agricultural border inside the country: passengers flying from Hawaii, Puerto Rico or the US Virgin Islands to the mainland cannot take most fresh fruit and vegetables, because of the risk of spreading invasive plant pests.

🛂 International flights: a second checklist

Passing TSA only answers the departure question. When you land in another country, customs asks something different: could this food carry a pest or an animal disease? That is why the last column of the table is mostly red for meat, dairy, eggs and fresh produce, and mostly green for packaged snacks.

Before you fly — checklist

1
Solid food goes in the carry-on with no limit. Anything spreadable or pourable over 100 ml goes in the checked bag.
2
Frozen food and ice packs must be frozen solid at the checkpoint.
3
Baby food, formula and pouches are exempt from the 100 ml rule. Tell the officer first.
4
Flying out of Hawaii, Puerto Rico or the US Virgin Islands: leave most fresh fruit and vegetables behind.
5
Flying abroad: check the destination’s customs rules for meat, dairy, eggs, fresh produce and honey. TSA approval means nothing there.
6
Bringing alcohol? See the duty-free alcohol allowance by country.

TSA rules apply to screening at US airports, and other countries’ security rules differ. Customs rules change and each authority has the final word, so confirm current requirements with the destination’s customs or agriculture authority before you travel. Updated 2026.
